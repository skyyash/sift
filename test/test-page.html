The Long Way Home

The 6:42 bus is never late, but it is never quick either. I used to spend the ride with my head down, reading one article start to finish and arriving with nothing to show for it. The problem was never the article. The problem was the screen, which handed me forty lines of text at once and punished me for looking up.

Every glance out the window cost me my place. A stop announcement, a sudden brake, or a stranger's elbow, and the paragraph I had been halfway through turned back into a grey wall. I would scan for a familiar word, pick the wrong one, and start the whole block over.

Reading in short steps changed that. One sentence at a time, large enough to read at arm's length, with everything I had already read resting quietly above it. When I looked away, nothing moved. When I looked back, the sentence was still waiting.

The same goes for notes. A thumb is a poor cursor, and nobody wants to drag tiny handles across a moving bus. A better gesture is a clumsy one: press, hold, let go. The sentence lands in a drawer, and the ride goes on.

By my stop, I had read the whole piece without ever feeling hurried. I had three lines saved and one question I wanted to chase later. That is a good commute.
